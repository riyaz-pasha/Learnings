# Coding Problem Study-Note Prompt (v2 — condensed)

Write a **single, compilable Java file** that's a standalone study note for this problem, from brute force to optimal — teach me *how to discover* the solution, not just the solution itself.

**Core requirement — the reasoning journey:**
Start by pinning down what's actually being asked and the binding constraint (usually the thing that explains why brute force falls over at scale) — a sentence or two, not a full requirements section. Then, for each approach, walk through: what I'd naturally try → why it works → why it's too slow → what work is repeated → what property/observation removes that bottleneck → how that leads to the next approach. Never jump straight to a named technique ("this is the classic two-pointer trick") — make me see *why* someone would think of it. Only include approaches that are meaningfully different — don't manufacture an extra step just to pad the journey.

**Also include, wherever it genuinely helps** (use judgment — skip anything that doesn't add value for this specific problem):
- The key insight, developed as a question I should learn to ask myself, with a correctness intuition ("this must work because..." not just "this seems to work")
- A couple of sharp examples (including one where a tempting approach fails), with ASCII diagrams or dry-run state tables if that's how the idea is best seen
- Real complexity reasoning (what's actually being visited/stored/repeated), not just the Big-O label
- The most tempting wrong approach or pitfall, and why it's tempting
- The reusable pattern: "when you see X, think Y" — plus 1-2 other problem types it transfers to
- How I'd talk through this out loud in an interview
- A couple of realistic interviewer follow-ups (changed constraints, weighted version, streaming/dynamic updates, etc. — only if relevant)

**Java code:** one file, clean idiomatic Java, modern collections (`Deque`, `PriorityQueue`, etc.) where they genuinely help, no cleverness for its own sake. Every non-trivial block gets a comment explaining the *reasoning* behind it — why this step, why this data structure, why this invariant holds — not a restatement of what the line does. A reader should be able to follow the logic and the "why" from the comments alone, without re-deriving it. Include a `main()` with representative + edge cases that cross-checks approaches against each other.

**End with a short summary:** core pattern, key observation, what's worth memorizing vs. understanding, most common trap, one-line mental trigger.

Be creative in how you teach this — vary the explanation style, diagrams, and depth to fit what actually makes *this* problem click. Don't force a rigid template.

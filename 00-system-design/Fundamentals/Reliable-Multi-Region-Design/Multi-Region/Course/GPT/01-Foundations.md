# Course: Evolving from Single-Region to Multi-Region Architecture

The goal of this course is to understand how to take a real production application running in one cloud region and evolve it into a geographically distributed system that can handle regional outages, serve users around the world, maintain data correctness, and meet regulatory requirements.

We will approach this from a backend and system design perspective, using examples involving application services, PostgreSQL, read replicas, Redis, message queues, object storage, load balancers, and AWS-style infrastructure. The principles apply to other cloud providers too.

# Chapter 1: Foundations — Understanding Multi-Region Architecture

## 1.1 What is a region?

A cloud region is a geographically separate area in which a cloud provider operates infrastructure and services.

For example, an application might run in:

- Mumbai, India
- Singapore
- Frankfurt, Germany
- Northern Virginia, United States

Each region has its own compute, networking, and regional service resources. Exact capabilities and service availability vary by cloud provider.

A region typically contains multiple Availability Zones (AZs). These are designed to provide failure isolation within a region.

The key distinction is:

- Multi-AZ protects against many infrastructure failures within a region.
- Multi-region adds protection against failures affecting an entire region and can reduce latency for geographically distributed users.

Multi-region architecture also introduces additional infrastructure, data synchronization, and operational complexity. It is not automatically the right choice for every application. AWS's architecture guidance similarly recommends evaluating whether multi-region is necessary when a well-designed multi-AZ deployment already meets the requirements.&#x20;

This architecture can scale horizontally inside the region. You can run multiple application instances, spread them across availability zones, and configure a highly available database.

However, suppose a serious regional incident makes the entire region unavailable.

Your application instances may be unreachable. Your database may be unavailable. Your workers may stop processing jobs. Regional endpoints may no longer work.

Having ten application instances instead of two does not solve a failure that affects all ten instances and their shared dependencies.

This is the fundamental reason to consider multi-region architecture.

## 1.3 Why evolve to multi-region?

There are four major reasons.

### A. Regional disaster recovery

Your business cannot tolerate losing access to the application for several hours when a cloud region fails.

You deploy a recovery environment in a second region and replicate critical data there.

The objective is to restore service within an agreed time and limit the amount of data lost.

Examples include payment platforms, critical business applications, and systems whose downtime causes substantial operational or financial damage.

### B. Lower latency for global users

Suppose your backend runs in India, but users also access it from Europe and North America.

If every request must travel to India, users far away may experience slower response times.

You can deploy application instances closer to users and route requests to suitable regions.

However, this works best when the request's critical dependencies are also nearby. Running an application server in Europe while every database query must travel to India may simply move the latency bottleneck elsewhere.

### C. High availability

A multi-region deployment can continue serving requests when one region becomes unavailable, provided that the surviving region has the necessary capacity, data, dependencies, and routing configuration.

Two regions do not automatically mean high availability. If both depend on a single-region database, authentication service, encryption key service, or other critical component, that component can remain a single point of failure.

### D. Data residency and regulatory requirements

Some businesses must restrict where specific categories of data are stored, processed, accessed, or transferred.

You may need to serve customers in multiple regions while keeping certain personal, financial, health, or government-related information within permitted geographic boundaries.

This requirement can affect the entire data flow, including databases, logs, backups, analytics, support access, and replicas. Merely deploying application servers in the required country is not sufficient.

We will examine these constraints separately in Chapter 8.

## 1.4 First principle: define the problem before choosing the architecture

A common mistake is to start with:

> We need multi-region, so let's deploy the application in two regions and configure a global load balancer.

This starts with a solution before establishing what the business actually requires.

Instead, define the required outcomes first.

Consider three hypothetical applications:

| Requirement           | Internal reporting tool                   | E-commerce application                           | Financial transaction platform                         |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Downtime tolerance    | Several hours                             | A few minutes                                    | Potentially very low                                   |
| Data loss tolerance   | Several hours of data might be acceptable | Depends on the operation                         | Often extremely low                                    |
| Global low latency    | Not essential                             | Useful                                           | Depends on the workload                                |
| Writes                | Can be temporarily unavailable            | Must preserve order and correctness where needed | Must preserve strict transaction invariants            |
| Likely starting point | Single region, multi-AZ                   | Multi-AZ plus regional DR                        | Multi-AZ with carefully designed cross-region recovery |

These are illustrative requirements, not universal recommendations. A financial platform may not need active-active writes, and an e-commerce platform may require strict consistency for inventory and payments even when other features can tolerate stale data.

The important point is that different parts of the same application can require different consistency and availability strategies.

For example, an e-commerce system could tolerate slightly stale product descriptions but cannot casually tolerate double-charging a customer.

## 1.5 Two critical metrics: RTO and RPO

Before designing failover, learn these two terms. They appear throughout disaster recovery planning.

### Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

RTO is the maximum acceptable duration for restoring a service after a disruption.

Suppose your application becomes unavailable at 10:00 AM.

Your business defines an RTO of 15 minutes.

That means the recovery process should restore the required service by approximately 10:15 AM.

RTO includes more than starting new application instances. Depending on your design, recovery may require detecting the incident, promoting a database replica, changing routing, scaling compute, restoring dependencies, and validating service health.

### Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

RPO is the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, expressed as a period of time.

Suppose your primary database fails at 10:00 AM, and the recovered database contains all committed changes only up to 9:58 AM.

Approximately two minutes of recent writes may be missing, so the recovery has an RPO of about two minutes in this example.

### How do RTO and RPO affect architecture?

| Strategy           | Typical recovery characteristic                                         | Main trade-off                                                                           |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Warm standby       | Faster recovery because a reduced environment is already running        | Higher ongoing cost and operational effort                                               |
| Active-passive     | Secondary region is ready but normally does not serve the main workload | Simpler write ownership, but failover still requires coordination                        |
| Active-active      | Multiple regions serve traffic continuously                             | Can reduce traffic recovery time but makes data correctness and conflict handling harder |

## 1.6 High availability vs. disaster recovery

These terms are related, but they solve different problems.

High availability (HA) is about keeping the service running when components fail.

Disaster recovery (DR) is about restoring the service and its data after a major disruption.

For example, suppose PostgreSQL is configured with a standby database in another availability zone. If the primary database fails, the standby can be promoted and the application can continue operating.

That is a high-availability mechanism within a region.

Now suppose the entire region becomes unavailable. You need a database and application environment in another region, plus a mechanism for recovering service there. That is a regional disaster recovery strategy.

This is a conceptual illustration, not a fully specified production architecture. The actual design must define which database can accept writes, how queues and files are recovered, and what happens when either region becomes unavailable.

The important lesson is that a region contains an entire dependency graph, not just a set of application servers.

For every critical dependency, ask:

- Can the application in Region B operate without Region A?
- Is the required data available in Region B?
- Can the application authenticate users and decrypt data?
- Are background jobs, queues, and scheduled tasks available?
- Can the system send notifications, process payments, or call external services?
- Can operators deploy and recover the system if the original region is unavailable?

A single critical dependency that exists only in the failed region may defeat your entire recovery plan.

## 1.8 Stateless versus stateful components

This distinction helps determine which parts of an application are easy to duplicate across regions.

### Stateless components

A stateless application server does not rely on local memory or disk to preserve essential user or business state between requests.

For example, an instance of a Spring Boot application can receive a request, validate the authentication token, query the appropriate data store, and return a response.

You can run many equivalent instances in different regions.

As long as their required configuration, dependencies, and data access remain available, the instances can process requests independently.

### Stateful components

Stateful components retain information that affects future operations.

Examples include:

- PostgreSQL databases
- Redis instances holding authoritative session or business state
- Message queues containing unprocessed messages
- Object storage containing uploaded documents
- Systems maintaining workflow progress

These components require explicit decisions about replication, persistence, ordering, ownership, and recovery.

A particularly important distinction is between a cache and a source of truth. A disposable product-catalog cache can usually be rebuilt from the database. A Redis instance holding the only copy of an important workflow state cannot safely be treated the same way.

| Application servers               | Yes                                       | Configuration, capacity and compatible dependencies       |
| Static assets                     | Often                                     | Replication or CDN availability                           |
| Read-only configuration           | Often                                     | Version consistency and secure distribution               |
| PostgreSQL                        | No                                        | Replication, write ownership and recovery                 |
| Redis                             | Depends on its role                       | Data loss, cache invalidation and consistency             |
| Message queue                     | Depends on the service and design         | Message durability, ordering and duplicate processing     |

This classification is an important step before attempting a multi-region migration.

Stage 1 — Make the existing region reliable. Use health checks, horizontal scaling, resilient database configuration, automated deployments, monitoring, and tested backups.

Stage 2 — Define recovery objectives. Agree on RTO and RPO with the business. Identify critical services, acceptable data loss, and the operations that must continue during a regional failure.

Stage 3 — Prepare another region. Deploy infrastructure through infrastructure as code, replicate or back up critical data, and validate networking, secrets, permissions, and service dependencies.

Stage 4 — Test recovery. Demonstrate that you can actually promote or restore the database, redirect traffic, process pending jobs, and recover the system within the required objectives.

Stage 5 — Introduce regional routing. Use global traffic management and health-based routing. Make sure the receiving region is capable of handling the workload, including after the primary region fails.

Stage 6 — Consider active-active serving. Only introduce multiple writable regions where the workload genuinely benefits and you have a defensible consistency and conflict-resolution strategy.

The sequence is not mandatory for every application, but it provides a sensible progression that avoids introducing distributed-system complexity before it is needed.

## 1.10 Chapter 1: What you should remember

The core ideas are:

1. Multi-region architecture is not simply a deployment topology. It is a business resilience, data management, and operational decision.
2. Multi-AZ and multi-region address different failure scopes.
3. RTO defines how quickly service must recover; RPO defines the acceptable amount of data loss.
4. Duplicating application servers is insufficient if the database or another critical dependency remains available only in the failed region.
5. Stateless application components are generally easier to distribute than stateful components.
6. Replicas help with availability and read scaling, but do not replace independent backups.
7. Active-active is not automatically better than active-passive. The correct design depends on recovery objectives, latency, consistency, compliance, cost, and operational capability.

The most important design rule: establish the required availability, data-loss tolerance, latency, and compliance guarantees first. Then choose the simplest architecture that satisfies them.
